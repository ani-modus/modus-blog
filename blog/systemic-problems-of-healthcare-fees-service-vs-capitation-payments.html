Systemic Problems Facing Injured Workers and Corporations

If some medical care is good, then surely more medical care is better, right? Unfortunately, this isn't how the system typically works. The rate of use of common medical services varies markedly across the United States; however, measures of health aren't better where services are liberally provided. In fact, studies report that in areas where people receive more services, the quality measures of health are actually lower.

There is a constant quest to understand how to integrate prevention and drive toward the mission of maintaining and enhancing patient safety, as opposed to applying a "quick fix."

In today's world, physicians are financially incentivized to overtreat patients. However, more treatment isn't always in the patient's best interest, and often leads to worse outcomes.

Monetary incentivization of overtreatment is increasingly recognized as a major cause of patient harm and excess cost. The American Institute of Medicine suggested that overprescribing "unnecessary services" is the largest financial burden on the US healthcare system, accounting for roughly $210 billion of the $750 billion spent in excess each year. The current American healthcare system doesn't penalize physicians for worse outcomes; instead, they're only monetarily incentivized to prescribe more treatment. This has led us to explore how prevention can be integrated into a payment model that benefits both patient and physician.

So, the question is: how?

First, we need to understand how to give better care when people are injured, whether the issue is work-related or not. Below are some of the most prominent systemic problems facing injured workers and corporations.

Coercion-Based Medicine: A Break of Oath?

Patients who are injured or sick experience a lot of anxiety and fear, some rational, most irrational. That fear is inevitable; most patients experience some level of distress when injured or newly diagnosed. However, some doctors use fear-based messaging when counseling patients about their conditions.

How often do doctors say things like "if you don't do this, you might have permanent nerve damage, end up in a wheelchair, have a stroke, die," and so on? Although there's no specific data quantifying how many doctors practice this style of communication, patients report that such conversations are relatively common in clinical encounters.

Finding a doctor who will truthfully advise on realistic treatment options is far better than one who tries to sell unnecessary procedures using fear. People take doctors very seriously, since they're highly regarded in society, and some doctors take advantage of that trust, prioritizing their own interests over their patients'.

Barriers to Choosing the Right Doctor

When looking for a reputable doctor, most people ask a trusted friend, neighbor, or colleague for a recommendation. Just as often, they search the way they'd book a hotel: through Google. But a Google search isn't always fair to the doctor. Some patients are thrilled by great customer service or a nice office, while others are disappointed if they don't get a quick fix. Neither type of review really indicates whether a doctor is aligned with your best interests. Online reviews are good for service-level feedback, not clinical outcomes.

"To give you an example, I practice 15 minutes south of Detroit, and I live about 30 minutes north of Detroit. I can tell you the best doctors in musculoskeletal care around where I practice, because I've dealt with them and seen their work product over the years. But I have no idea which one to choose where I live, and I'm an insider within the industry. So it's very difficult for everyone, even people working within healthcare." Ali Alhimiri, MD

This doesn't only affect patients. It complicates things for employers too, who struggle to decide which physicians or healthcare services to refer employees to. As Nic Patee put it: "A multi-state, multinational corporation with 100,000 employees scattered throughout the US would need to understand those marketplaces in every single city. That's a considerable challenge."

The Negative Impact of Fee-for-Service

The way we pay for health care is "a pain point for everyone," according to Ali Alhimiri, MD, founder of Modus. For patients, about a third of bankruptcies are related to medical bills. Employers who pay for care, and the clinicians treating these patients daily, all have to chase bills and make sure they're getting paid and staying in business. Most commonly, we pay for healthcare via fee-for-service, meaning the more doctors and providers do, the more they get paid.

Fee-for-service is the most traditional payment model in healthcare, reimbursing physicians based on the volume of services they provide. It rewards quantity over quality.

In our view, a fee-for-service system discourages long-term engagement with a patient due to its transactional nature, while capitation encourages less care for more profit. A hybrid of both has the best chance of balancing access, quality, and cost.

There's no reward for a patient's overall, long-term outcome. Instead, physicians are rewarded for winning the volume race, and the system penalizes doctors who provide thoughtful, conservative care.

Administrative Burden on Physicians

Today, this system treats all clinicians the same way. Every doctor gets paid the same way and has to navigate the same cumbersome payer pre-authorization process. This creates a lot of frustration.

Physicians interacting with the US healthcare system encounter unnecessary administrative complexity at every turn: unwieldy electronic health records (EHR) systems, redundant text-generated documentation, insurance pre-authorization, and the games of billing, all just to get reimbursed. This creates a tedious process for physicians and a considerable financial burden on the system.

According to the Center for American Progress, the US spends nearly $500 billion on billing and insurance-related costs. Compared to Canada, reports indicate the US has 44% more administrative staff, and US physicians spend nearly 50% more time on administrative tasks. One study found that, on average, a US physician spends half their workday on the EHR, including billing, coding, ordering, and communication.

Opportunities to Improve the Healthcare System

Guiding Patients to the Right Doctors

Finding a doctor you can trust is genuinely invaluable. It creates a sense of well-being, and it's a meaningful gesture for employers to help their people find qualified physicians.

What patients need is a thoughtful doctor with a healthy inkling of doubt, someone who has genuinely thought through the treatment options available. We often hear about "the benefits outweighing the risks." That's where we come in: to tell you which doctors have consistently followed best-practice standards to help patients actually get better, not just to provide more care regardless of outcome.

"Occasionally you hear a doctor say, 'I did this surgery because it was the last resort,' which makes no sense, because it's the equivalent of hiring an arsonist to burn down your house to get rid of pests, leaving you homeless with a bigger problem than you started with. Calling something a last resort implies it would be beneficial, and a lot of the time, it isn't. The right philosophy is treating someone because the benefit genuinely outweighs the risk and harm of the treatment." Ali Alhimiri, MD

Modus Clinicians' Responsible Autonomy

One of the most effective strategies for curtailing this financial drain and poor quality is a population-based hybrid of capitation and fee-for-service (FFS). A capitation fee pays doctors a fixed amount per patient. The capitation model creates incentives for efficacy, preventive care, and cost control, while FFS helps guard against withholding care.

One key benefit of capitation is lower overhead and administrative cost for clinicians, since they don't have to wait to be reimbursed for each service, freeing the practice to focus on better patient care. At the same time, a lower FFS component keeps patients serviced promptly and encourages access.

However, capitation has its own flaws: it can incentivize doctors to enroll as many patients as possible, then withhold necessary care to keep costs down. None of that benefits the patient. If the system monitored patient function as closely as cost, this could be prevented.

Building Trust Through Transparency

We need a system of trust: one with a trust score that rewards doctors for their ability to provide reliable, quality care, and to minimize poor functional outcomes and unnecessary expense.
