Most provider ratings are built from things that are easy to measure, not things that matter. Star ratings, self-reported specialties, patient satisfaction surveys with a five percent response rate. None of it tells you whether a doctor's judgment, on the decisions that actually shape a patient's outcome, holds up.

A Modus Score is built from seven signals instead, each one read from real Medicare claims data, each one peer-matched to the same specialty and state, and each one focused specifically on elective, expensive procedures, the category where the decision to operate is itself the judgment being measured.

Why elective procedures, specifically

An emergency surgery doesn't tell you much about a physician's judgment. The decision was made for them. An elective procedure is different: the physician chose to recommend it, chose the approach, and owns the outcome. That's where practice pattern actually shows up in the data, and it's why the seven signals are read exclusively from that category rather than averaged across everything a physician does.

The seven signals

Each signal is an event, not a code, meaning it reflects something that measurably happened to a patient, not just what was billed.

  • Rate of surgery, among new and existing patients, a read on how readily this physician reaches for the operating room.
  • Short- and long-acting opioid prescription rates, cost management for prescribing that's owned by the person writing it.
  • Utilization of expensive in-office testing, the same logic applied to diagnostics.
  • Postoperative hospital days, the difference between a one-day recovery and a one-month one.
  • Surgical revision rates on the same body part, the clearest signal there is: the operation that had to be done twice.
  • One-year postoperative mortality, clinical judgment on high-risk patients, whether to operate at all.
  • Standing of federal and state licenses, and litigation history, verified at the source, not assumed from a specialty listing.

Every one of these is peer-matched, same specialty, same state, so a spine surgeon in a rural market is never compared against national averages that don't reflect their actual patient population or case complexity.

What the score deliberately leaves out

No self-reported outcomes. No patient satisfaction scores, which correlate more closely with bedside manner and wait times than with whether the procedure actually worked. No risk adjustment on the seven core signals, because risk adjustment would smooth over the exact decision the rating is meant to surface: whether to operate at all, given this patient's risk profile.

That last point is worth sitting with. Risk adjustment is standard practice in most quality measurement, and for good reason in most contexts. But if the question is "did this physician make the right call on a borderline case," adjusting away the risk profile removes the very thing you're trying to measure.

Two grades, and an honest blank

The seven signals resolve into a rating, and only four- and five-star physicians are approached for a direct contract. But Modus deliberately publishes only two outcomes for a physician who doesn't clear that bar: a passing grade, or unrated. There's no public "poor" grade, and that's intentional. A physician with limited claims history, a new specialty focus, or simply not enough data yet isn't the same as a physician with a real quality problem, and collapsing those two into one bad rating would be dishonest in the other direction.

Unrated isn't a verdict. It's a status with a published, defined path out of it: connect a live outcomes feed, and get measured over time like everyone else. That's the whole point of building the score from events instead of self-reports in the first place, it holds up to being checked, including when the honest answer is "we don't know yet."
